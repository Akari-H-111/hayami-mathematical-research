\begin{abstract}
We reconstruct the orthogonal-circle ruled surface from the hash-bound v4
manuscript and correct its boundary and signal statements. A smooth atlas
retains the entire closed parameter domain, including the two $Q=0$ edges.
Its surface map loses rank at three angular-chart points and two additional
boundary corners. The interior observation field has two nondegenerate
lateral zeros and a degenerate polar zero. Its critical set includes a
symmetry branch omitted from the old rational parametrization, with one
exceptional point that is not a fold. We prove the physical rational-fold
domain and the complete skeleton--critical intersection statement.
Transverse crossing alone does not force recurrence. In actual fold charts,
recurrence is characterized by equality of the tangential coordinates and
equality up to sign of the normal coordinates; a repeated nonzero intrinsic
observation survives an external rotation precisely when the phases lock.
Squared-cosine frequency doubling is a conditional response in chosen target
coordinates, not a trajectory-independent law. Exact replay and a separate
Lean companion distinguish formal theorems, manuscript proofs, background
references, and independent open research questions. Each asserted result
has claimwise Lean coverage in the new companion.
\end{abstract}

\begin{definition}[Paired circles and interior surface map]\label{def:surface}
Set $c=\cos t$, $s=\sin t$, $Q=\sqrt{c(2-c)}$ and
\[
 C_1(t)=(c,s,0),\qquad C_2(\varphi)=(1+\cos\varphi,0,\sin\varphi),
 \qquad c+\cos\varphi=1,\quad \sin\varphi\geq0.
\]
For $-\pi/2\leq t\leq\pi/2$ and $0\leq u\leq1$, the continuous surface
map is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:surface}
 S(t,u)=\big(c+2u(1-c),(1-u)s,uQ\big).
\end{equation}
Its angular chart is restricted to $-\pi/2<t<\pi/2$, where $Q>0$.
The closed domain receives its smooth structure from that chart and the
boundary charts below, rather than from the old closed $t$ rectangle.
Equivalently it is the paired-angle curve
$\{(t,\varphi):\cos t+\cos\varphi=1,\ -\pi/2\leq t\leq\pi/2,
0\leq\varphi\leq\pi/2\}$, times $[0,1]$.
The constraint has nonzero gradient everywhere on this curve:
at its two endpoints $\sin t\ne0$, and at $t=0$ one has
$\sin\varphi=1$. Thus this is the usual smooth structure of the pairing
curve. Near its endpoints $r=\sin\varphi$ is a regular coordinate.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Boundary atlas without discarding the edges]\label{thm:atlas}
For $\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}$ and $0\leq r<1/2$, put
\[
 c_r=1-\sqrt{1-r^2},\quad s_r=\varepsilon\sqrt{1-c_r^2},\qquad
 S_\varepsilon(r,u)=\big(c_r+2u(1-c_r),(1-u)s_r,ur\big).
\]
Together with the angular chart these maps describe the entire original
closed parameter domain as a smooth manifold with corners, and the surface
map extends smoothly in every chart. On the overlaps,
$r=Q(t)$, $t=\varepsilon\arccos c_r$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transition}
 \frac{dr}{dt}=-\frac{s(1-c)}{Q}\ne0.
\end{equation}
At the $r=0$ edge,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary-derivative}
 (S_\varepsilon)_r=(0,0,u),\quad
 (S_\varepsilon)_u=(2,-\varepsilon,0),\quad
 (S_\varepsilon)_r\times(S_\varepsilon)_u=(\varepsilon u,2u,0).
\end{equation}
The boundary surface differential is injective exactly when $u\ne0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The two radicands are positive for $|r|<1/2$, so the displayed functions
extend smoothly to an open neighborhood across $r=0$ and across each
$u$ endpoint. Also $c_r(2-c_r)=r^2$ and $c_r^2+s_r^2=1$.
For $r>0$ the inverse formula matches the sign of $t$ and gives the original
circle pairing; both transition maps are smooth, with nonzero derivative
\eqref{eq:transition}. The angular chart covers the remaining part of the
domain, including $t=0$. Thus no original surface points are removed.
At $r=0$, $c'_r=s'_r=0$, which proves \eqref{eq:boundary-derivative}.
For $u\ne0$ the two displayed vectors are independent; for $u=0$ the
first is zero and the second is nonzero, giving rank one.
A single global parameter makes the manifold assembly explicit. For
$-1\leq v\leq1$ define
\begin{align*}
 c_g(v)&=(1-v^2)^2,\\
 s_g(v)&=v\sqrt{(2-v^2)(1+(1-v^2)^2)},\\
 q_g(v)&=(1-v^2)\sqrt{2-c_g(v)},\\
 t_g(v)&=2\arcsin\!\left(v\sqrt{1-v^2/2}\right).
\end{align*}
Both square-root radicands in $s_g,q_g$ are strictly positive on this
interval. Their exact identities are $c_g^2+s_g^2=1$,
$q_g^2=c_g(2-c_g)$, and $(\cos t_g,\sin t_g,Q(t_g))=(c_g,s_g,q_g)$.
Thus substituting these functions into \eqref{eq:surface} gives exactly
the original surface. The function $t_g$ is continuous and strictly
increasing, has endpoint values $\pm\pi/2$, and hence is a homeomorphism
between the two closed base intervals. Its derivative is positive for
$|v|<1$, while $q'_g(\pm1)=\mp2\sqrt2\ne0$. Therefore its angular and
endpoint transitions have smooth local inverses and give the same atlas.
The product $[-1,1]\times[0,1]$ carries the standard product of interval
manifolds with boundary; the displayed surface is a smooth map on this
genuine manifold with corners. These assertions, including the global
homeomorphism and actual manifold smoothness, have corresponding Lean
theorems rather than merely a list of coordinate formulas.

\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Boundary corner obstruction]\label{prop:corner}
At either $(r,u)=(0,0)$ the image cannot be regularized as a $C^1$ embedded
surface with a continuous tangent plane containing its neighboring regular
patches. The rank loss is therefore not merely the old $1/Q$ formula.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $V_r=(S_\varepsilon)_r\times(S_\varepsilon)_u$.
For $0<r<1/2$, exact differentiation uses
$c'_r=r/\sqrt{1-r^2}$ and
$s'_r=-\varepsilon c_r r/(\sqrt{1-c_r^2}\sqrt{1-r^2})$.
Dividing the actual cross product by $r$ and taking the one-sided limit gives
\[
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}\frac{V_r(r,0)}r=(0,0,-\varepsilon),\qquad
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}\frac{V_r(r,r)}r=(\varepsilon,2,-\varepsilon).
\]
The limiting normal lines differ and both paths consist of regular points
for sufficiently small $r>0$. A $C^1$ embedded surface containing these
patches would have the same limiting tangent plane along both paths, a
contradiction. This is a local image obstruction; it is not a classification
of all global self-intersections.
An equivalent obstruction avoids choosing a normalization of $V$.
Direct differentiation gives
\[
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}S_r(r,0)/r=(1,0,0),\qquad
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}S_r(r,r)/r=(1,0,1),\qquad
 \lim_{r\downarrow0}S_u(r,0)=(2,-\varepsilon,0).
\]
A nonzero continuous local normal to a containing $C^1$ embedded patch
must annihilate these three independent limiting directions. This forces
all three components of that normal at the corner to vanish, contradicting
regularity. More directly, let a $C^1$ ambient chart $\chi$ flatten a
containing surface into $z=0$ and set $g=\chi_3$. The chain rule on the
physical quadrant (with one-sided derivatives at $u=0$) makes $Dg$
annihilate both actual surface tangents. The three limits force $Dg=0$
at the corner, whereas invertibility of $D\chi$ makes its third row
nonzero. The formal theorem uses any $0<\delta\leq1/2$, with
$0<r<\delta$ and $0\leq u\leq\delta$; it does not require a chart
covering the whole surface.

\end{proof}

\section{Intrinsic differential geometry and global rank classification}
All formulas in this section that contain $Q^{-1}$ use the angular chart
$Q>0$. We reserve $F_{\mathrm I}$ for the mixed metric coefficient to avoid
confusion with the observation field.
\begin{proposition}[Differential, metric, and non-developability]\label{prop:metric}
The tangent vectors and mixed derivative are
\[
 S_t=((2u-1)s,(1-u)c,us(c-1)/Q),\quad S_u=(2(1-c),-s,Q),
 \qquad S_{tu}=(2s,-c,s(c-1)/Q).
\]
The scalar triple product is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:triple}
 T=\det(S_t,S_u,S_{tu})=-\Pcore(c)s/Q,\qquad
 \Pcore(c)=c^2-3c+1.
\end{equation}
The first fundamental form is
\begin{align*}
 G&=2c^2-6c+5,\\
 F_{\mathrm I}&=s[(3-2c)u-(2-c)],\\
 E&=1+(-4s^2-2c^2)u+
       \left(4s^2+c^2+\frac{s^2(1-c)^2}{Q^2}\right)u^2.
\end{align*}
For $0\leq c\leq1$, $1\leq G\leq5$, with $G(1)=1$ and $G(0)=5$.
At regular angular-chart points the Gaussian curvature is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvature}
 K=-\frac{T^2}{\lVert S_t\times S_u\rVert^4}\leq0.
\end{equation}
It is strictly negative except where $s\Pcore(c)=0$; in particular the
surface is non-developable. Curvature agrees on the actual angular--boundary
atlas overlaps, and each regular $r=0$ edge has $K=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate $Q^2=c(2-c)$ to obtain $Q_t=s(c-1)/Q$ and the displayed
vectors. Dot products give $E,F_{\mathrm I},G$. The bounds follow from
$G-1=(1-c)(4-2c)$ and $5-G=c(6-2c)$.
For the triple product, write $S=C_1+u d$. The term containing $u d'$ in
$\det(C'_1+ud',d,d')$ vanishes. Expansion reduces the remaining
numerator to
$s[c^2(2-c)-(1-c)^3]=-s\Pcore(c)$.
At a regular point let $n=(S_t\times S_u)/\lVert S_t\times S_u\rVert$.
The $uu$ coefficient of the second fundamental form is zero because
$S_{uu}=0$, and its mixed coefficient is $n\cdot S_{tu}=T/\lVert
S_t\times S_u\rVert$. The Gram determinant of the first form is
$\lVert S_t\times S_u\rVert^2$. Substitution in the definition of
Gaussian curvature proves \eqref{eq:curvature}. In the physical angular
interval its zero directions are exactly $t=0,\pm t_0$, excluding the rank
singularities where $K$ is undefined. The actual regular point
$(t,u)=(\pi/3,0)$ has $K<0$, proving non-developability.
On a separated atlas transition $(r,u)\mapsto(T(r),u)$ with
$\alpha=T'(r)\ne0$, the first tangents transform as
$(S_r,S_u)=(\alpha S_t,S_u)$ and the second derivatives as
$(S_{rr},S_{ru},S_{uu})=(\alpha^2S_{tt}+T''S_t,\alpha S_{tu},S_{uu})$.
The normal annihilates the added tangential term. The numerator and
denominator of $K$ both acquire the factor $\alpha^4$, proving
consistency on the actual atlas overlaps. At $r=0$, direct differentiation
gives $S_{ru}=(0,0,1)$ and $S_{uu}=0$, while the regular area vector
$(\varepsilon u,2u,0)$ is orthogonal to $S_{ru}$. Thus $K=0$ on both
regular boundary edges; their $u=0$ corners have no regular curvature.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Five rank singularities on the completed domain]\label{thm:regularity}
The surface differential loses rank at precisely the polar point $(t,u)=(0,1)$,
the two lateral points $(t,u)=(\pm t_0,u_0)$, and the two boundary
corners $(r,u)=(0,0)$, where
\[
 c_0=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2},\quad t_0=\arccos c_0,\quad
 u_0=\frac{1-c_0}{2}=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{4}.
\]
Every other point of the completed parameter domain is an immersion point.
This corrects the three-point global assertion of v4 Theorem 2.3.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
In the angular chart direct cross multiplication gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cross}
 V=S_t\times S_u=\left(
 \frac{c^2(2-c)-u(1-c+c^2)}Q,
 -\frac{s[2u-c(2-c)]}Q,
 (1-c)(1-c-2u)\right).
\end{equation}
At $c=1$ one has $t=s=0$ and $V=(1-u,0,0)$, so precisely $u=1$
loses rank. At $0<c<1$, $s\ne0$. The last component forces
$u=(1-c)/2$, and the second then forces $\Pcore(c)=0$.
For this $u$ the first numerator is
$-(1+c)\Pcore(c)/2$, so no additional condition remains.
$\Pcore$ decreases strictly on $[0,1]$, has opposite endpoint signs,
and its only root there is $c_0$. The cosine equation gives exactly the
two stated angular points. The boundary charts are classified by
Theorem~\ref{thm:atlas}. A two-dimensional differential into $\R^3$ is
injective exactly when the cross product of its basis images is nonzero,
which completes the argument.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
At $u=1$, the two labels $t$ and $-t$ have the same image on $C_2$.
In particular both $Q=0,u=1$ points map to $(2,0,0)$. Immersion of a
labelled branch does not imply a global embedding or identify the two
parameter points. We do not quotient these labels in the domain.
\end{remark}

\section{Interior observation field, skeleton, and zeros}
\begin{definition}[Fixed angular-chart observation field]\label{def:observation}
On $Q>0$ define
\[
 N=(1-c)(1-c-2u),\quad H=c^2(2-c)-uD(c),\quad
 D(c)=1-c+c^2,\quad M=H/Q,
\]
and $\Map(t,u)=(N,M)$, equivalently $F=N+iM$.
For the rotated coordinate projection $(X_\theta,Y_\theta)
=(x\cos\theta+z\sin\theta,y)$, its Jacobian is
$J_\theta=N\cos\theta-M\sin\theta$.
This projection Jacobian is distinct from $\det D\Map$ below.
\end{definition}
The projection identity follows by bilinearity from the $z$ and $x$
components of \eqref{eq:cross}. These components are coordinate area
densities. On a boundary overlap,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:field-transform}
 F_r=\frac{dt}{dr}F_t=-\frac{r}{s(1-c)}F_t.
\end{equation}
For fixed $u>0$, $M_t\sim-u/r$ diverges, whereas the new chart has
$F_r\longrightarrow i\varepsilon u$ at its edge. Thus the full surface
is retained, but this fixed $F_t$ and its fold set are not asserted to
extend as the same global scalar observation. Chart-dependent field
zeros and critical points must not be silently identified across $Q=0$.

\begin{proposition}[Skeleton and complete zero set]\label{prop:zeros}
The interior skeleton $\{M=0\}$ is
\[
 u_{\rm skel}(c)=\frac{c^2(2-c)}{D(c)},\qquad
 D(c)=(c-1/2)^2+3/4>0.
\]
It lies in $[0,1]$ for $0\leq c\leq1$. The zeros of $\Map$ coincide
with the angular-chart rank singularities: two lateral zeros and the
polar zero $(0,1)$. Along the skeleton $F$ is real wherever it is
nonzero. Off it, $M$ has opposite signs on its two sides; neither side
is a region of constant phase $0$ or $\pi$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Solve $H=0$ using $D>0$. Its numerator is nonnegative and
$D-c^2(2-c)=(1-c)^2(1+c)\geq0$, giving the range.
The system $N=H=0$ at $c\ne1$ gives $u=(1-c)/2$ and then
$H=-(1+c)\Pcore(c)/2=0$. At $c=1$ it gives $u=1$.
The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:regularity} shows that the middle area
component vanishes at those same points. Finally $H$ is strictly
decreasing in $u$, so $M>0$ below and $M<0$ above the skeleton.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Lateral dipole with a separate polar zero]\label{thm:dipole}
Writing $s_0=\sin t_0>0$ and $Q_0=\sqrt{c_0(2-c_0)}>0$, one has
\[
 \det D\Map(\pm t_0,u_0)=\mp\frac{5s_0(1-c_0)^2}{Q_0}.
\]
Both lateral zeros are nondegenerate, with local indices $-1,+1$
in the ordered coordinates $(t,u)$ and target $(N,M)$, so their total
charge is zero. The polar zero has zero Jacobian and is on the domain
boundary $u=1$; no interior local-degree index is assigned to it here.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate $N,H,Q$ in the positive-$Q$ chart. At the lateral zero,
using $c_0^2=3c_0-1$ gives
\[
 N_t=s(1-c_0),\quad N_u=-2(1-c_0),\quad
 M_u=-2c_0/Q_0,\quad M_t=-s(5-7c_0)/(2Q_0).
\]
The determinant simplifies to $-5s(1-c_0)^2/Q_0$. The field is even
in $t$, so the determinants at the two points have opposite signs.
Here the planar local index means the winding number of the actual
nonzero observation on all sufficiently small positively oriented source
circles. For a closed nonzero complex loop $\gamma$, normalize it by
$\gamma(0)$ and lift that loop through $\exp:\C\to\C\setminus\{0\}$
with initial value zero. Its winding is the imaginary endpoint divided by
$2\pi$. Covering-space lifting proves that this is an integer, independent
of a chosen initial logarithm, and invariant under closed-loop homotopies.

Write the actual observation near a zero as $F(p+h)=Lh+R(h)$, where
$L$ is its invertible real derivative and $R(h)=o(\lVert h\rVert)$.
For all sufficiently small $h\ne0$,
$\lVert R(h)\rVert<\lVert h\rVert/(2\lVert L^{-1}\rVert)$,
so $Lh+\tau R(h)$ never vanishes for $0\leq\tau\leq1$.
This homotopes every sufficiently small actual observation circle to its
linear image. Normalize that image by $L(1)$: it becomes
$\cos\theta+\beta\sin\theta$, where $\beta=L(i)/L(1)$ and
$\operatorname{Im}\beta=\det L/|L(1)|^2$.
Deforming $\beta$ to $\varepsilon i$, with
$\varepsilon=\operatorname{sign}\det L$, preserves this imaginary sign
and hence nonvanishing. The resulting exponential circle has winding
$\varepsilon$. All source circles are chosen inside the physical interior
by reducing their radius once more. This proves the indices $-1,+1$
from genuine winding, rather than defining them as determinant signs.
These lifting, homotopy, linear-circle, and actual-field application
statements are proved in Lean. The convention agrees with the standard
planar index discussed in \cite{degree}; no unproved local-index theorem
is a formal dependency.
At $(0,1)$ both field components vanish and the Jacobian below has its
$s=0$ factor, proving degeneracy.
\end{proof}

\section{The full physical critical locus and its folds}
Set
\[
 A(c)=(1-c)c(2-c)(1+2c-c^2),\quad B(c)=c^3-3c+1,
\]
\[
 p_b(c)=c^5-5c^4+6c^3-c^2+c-1=-A(c)-B(c).
\]
\begin{theorem}[Critical locus with the missing symmetry branch]\label{thm:critical}
In the angular chart,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobian}
 J_{\Map}=-\frac{2s}{Q^3}\,[A(c)+uB(c)].
\end{equation}
Let $c_b$ be the unique root of $p_b$ in $[0,1]$; then
$613/1000<c_b<614/1000$. In the physical domain $0\leq u\leq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:critical}
 J_{\Map}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 t=0\quad\text{or}\quad
 [\,c_b\leq c\leq1,\ u=u_{\rm fold}(c):=-A(c)/B(c)\,].
\end{equation}
The rational branch reaches $u=1$ at $c_b$ and $u=0$ at $c=1$.
The root $c_*=2\cos(4\pi/9)$ of $B$ in $(0,1)$ is an asymptote of
the algebraic rational expression, not a critical point in the physical
domain. The rational formula alone is not the full critical locus.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The quotient rule with $Q_t=s(c-1)/Q$ gives
\eqref{eq:jacobian}. For $0<c<1$, $A>0$. If $A+uB=0$ with
$0\leq u\leq1$, necessarily $B<0$ and $p_b=-A-B\geq0$.
Conversely if $B<0$, the solution $u=-A/B$ lies in $[0,1]$ precisely
when $p_b\geq0$. At $c=1$, $A=0,B=-1$, giving $u=0$.
The derivative of $p_b$ is positive on $[0,1]$, as the following exact
Bernstein expression shows:
\[
 p'_b(c)=(1-c)^4+2c(1-c)^3+18c^2(1-c)^2+14c^3(1-c)+2c^4>0.
\]
Thus its opposite endpoint signs give a unique root, and exact endpoint
evaluation gives the stated rational interval. For $c\geq613/1000$,
$B<0$ (or directly use $B'=3c^2-3<0$ and $B(613/1000)<0$).
This proves the equivalence and endpoints. The factor $s=0$ in this
angular interval means $t=0$ and supplies the omitted branch.
$B$ is strictly decreasing on $(0,1)$ and the triple-angle identity
$B(2\cos\alpha)=2\cos3\alpha+1$ gives $c_*$.
At $c=c_*$ one has $A>0$, so no $u$ solves the fold equation there.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Ordinary folds and the exceptional point]\label{thm:fold}
Every physical critical point other than $(t,u)=(0,0)$ has actual smooth
source and target local charts, with smooth two-sided inverses, taking
$\Map$ to $(\xi,\eta)\mapsto(\xi,\eta^2)$.
At $(0,0)$ the ordinary fold criterion fails and the germ is not a fold.
This statement uses the extension to open angular-chart neighborhoods;
at $u=0$ or $u=1$ it does not claim a full two-sided physical preimage.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the symmetry branch $(0,u_*)$, $u_*>0$, explicit charts are
\[
 (\xi,\eta)=\left(1-u_*-M(t,u),
        2\sin(t/2)\sqrt{u-(1-c)/2}\right),\quad
 T(N,M)=(1-u_*-M,-N).
\]
The radicand is positive nearby, the source determinant at $(0,u_*)$
is $-\sqrt{u_*}\ne0$, and the target determinant is $-1$.
The exact identity $1-c=2\sin^2(t/2)$ gives $T\Map=(\xi,\eta^2)$.
The smooth inverse function theorem supplies both inverses.

On the rational branch, $N_u=-2(1-c)\ne0$ and the kernel vector is
$v=(N_u,-N_t)$. Direct differentiation on the branch gives
\[
 dJ_{\Map}(v)=-\frac{4s^2(c-1)R_7(c)}{Q^3B(c)},
\]
where
\[
 R_7=3c^7-8c^6-12c^5+60c^4-69c^3+30c^2-6c-1.
\]
It never vanishes on $[0,1]$, since
\begin{align*}
 -R_7(c)={}&(1-c)^7+13c(1-c)^6+27c^2(1-c)^5
             +44c^3(1-c)^4\\
          &+71c^4(1-c)^3+57c^5(1-c)^2+21c^6(1-c)+3c^7>0.
\end{align*}
The map is smooth and has rank one. The kernel is transverse to its
critical locus, so the smooth fold recognition theorem applies
\cite[Theorem 3.4(1)]{ishikawa}. These are its actual hypotheses, not
only smoothness of the critical curve. Independently, the pinned Lean
dependency constructs the source and target charts and both inverses
for this same physical branch; it does not postulate the recognition
theorem as an axiom.

At $(0,0)$, the derivative of $\Map$ is $(v_t,v_u)\mapsto(0,-v_u)$,
whereas $dJ_{\Map}=0$. A fold has nonzero kernel derivative of its
Jacobian. This property is invariant under smooth invertible coordinate
changes (at a critical point the Jacobian is multiplied by nonzero
factors). Hence this germ is not a fold. Its further classification is
not asserted in this paper.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Skeleton--critical intersection]\label{thm:intersection}
The entire physical intersection is the polar point $(0,1)$ plus the two
rational-branch points with $t=\pm\arccos c_{**}$,
$u=u_{\rm skel}(c_{**})$, where
\[
 E_5(c)=c^5-3c^4+5c^3-6c^2+c+1,\qquad
 673741/10^6<c_{**}<673742/10^6.
\]
There is exactly one root $c_{**}$ of $E_5$ on the physical rational-fold
interval. These are observation-fold intersections; neither rational
intersection is a surface immersion singularity.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On the rational branch $D>0$, $B<0$, and $c(2-c)>0$.
Multiplying $u_{\rm skel}=u_{\rm fold}$ by these nonzero factors gives
\[
 cB+(1-c)(1+2c-c^2)D=E_5(c)=0.
\]
On $[3/5,1]$ the derivative of $E_5$ is negative. For example set
$x=(5c-3)/2$; its negative derivative is
\[
 \frac{343}{125}(1-x)^4+\frac{1504}{125}x(1-x)^3+
 \frac{474}{25}x^2(1-x)^2+\frac{64}{5}x^3(1-x)+3x^4>0.
\]
The exact signs at the two isolating endpoints imply existence and
uniqueness on $[c_b,1]$. The physical critical classification excludes
any other rational intersection. On $t=0$, $M=1-u$, so only $u=1$
lies on the skeleton. Finally $c_{**}>0.673$ while $c_0<0.383$, and
$t\ne0$ at the rational intersections, excluding every surface rank
singularity. The old name ``absolute singularity'' conflated these
different kinds of degeneracy and is not used as a surface claim here.
\end{proof}

\section{Signal response and a proved replacement for Theorem 5.3}
\begin{proposition}[Local squared-cosine response]\label{prop:response}
In a fixed pair of Whitney charts choose a trajectory with
$\xi=\xi_0\ne0$ and $\eta(\tau)=a\cos(\omega\tau)$ whose entire
range stays in the chosen chart. Define the chosen target-coordinate
phase, using a fixed arctangent branch, by
\[
 \phi_W(\tau)=\arctan\left(\frac{\eta(\tau)^2}{\xi_0}\right),\qquad
 \kappa=a^2/\xi_0.
\]
For $\omega\ne0$ this response has period $\pi/|\omega|$ (not
necessarily its least period when $a=0$), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:response-bound}
 \left|\phi_W(\tau)-\frac\kappa2[1+\cos(2\omega\tau)]\right|
       \leq |\kappa|^3/3.
\end{equation}
For $0<|\kappa|\leq1/2$, the leading nonconstant harmonic is at
$2|\omega|$; the constant term is retained. These claims concern
$\phi_W$, not the phase of a general physical signal under a nonlinear
target chart or an additional rotation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Use $\cos^2 x=(1+\cos2x)/2$ and
$\cos(x+\pi)^2=\cos^2x$. The derivatives of
$x-\arctan x$ and $x^3/3-x+\arctan x$ are respectively
$x^2/(1+x^2)$ and $x^4/(1+x^2)$, both nonnegative.
Both functions vanish at zero. Monotonicity for $x\geq0$, followed by
oddness for $x<0$, therefore proves
$|\arctan x-x|\leq |x|^3/3$.
Since $|\kappa\cos^2(\omega\tau)|\leq|\kappa|$, the bound follows.
Half-period invariance excludes odd harmonics relative to $\omega$.
For a concrete leading-harmonic statement, the uniform remainder has
real sine/cosine coefficients of magnitude at most $2|\kappa|^3/3$.
The $2\omega$ cosine coefficient differs from $\kappa/2$ by at most
that amount; all higher nonconstant coefficients have that bound, and
the sine coefficients vanish by evenness. When $|\kappa|\leq1/2$,
$|\kappa|/2-2|\kappa|^3/3>2|\kappa|^3/3$.
Thus the fundamental doubled cosine term dominates the higher ones.
No general spectrum for an arbitrary transverse trajectory follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exact conditional recurrence]\label{thm:recurrence}
Let $\Map$ have an ordinary fold at $p$, with source chart
$\sigma=(\xi,\eta)$ and invertible target chart $T$. In a sufficiently
small common neighborhood, any two points $p_1,p_2$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fiber}
 \Map(p_1)=\Map(p_2)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \xi(p_1)=\xi(p_2),\quad
 [\eta(p_1)=\eta(p_2)\ \text{or}\ \eta(p_1)=-\eta(p_2)].
\end{equation}
In particular a trajectory containing two distinct-time points with
the same $\xi$ and opposite nonzero $\eta$ retraces an observation.
These are sufficient conditions by a proved equality, not by transverse
crossing alone.

For the explicitly defined rotated observation signal
\[
 Z(\tau)=F(\gamma(\tau)),\qquad
 \Psi(\tau)=e^{i\theta(\tau)}Z(\tau),
\]
if $Z(\tau_1)=Z(\tau_2)=z\ne0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase-lock}
 \Psi(\tau_1)=\Psi(\tau_2)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \theta(\tau_1)-\theta(\tau_2)\in2\pi\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
For $z=0$ no phase lock is needed. For uniform rotation this condition is
$\omega_0(\tau_1-\tau_2)\in2\pi\mathbb Z$.
If an alternative amplitude/phase readout is used, it must first be
specified as a single-valued function of $\Map$ for its equalities to
be transported from \eqref{eq:fiber}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Shrink the source and target neighborhoods so both chart inverses
hold there. Then $T\Map= (\xi,\eta^2)$ and injectivity of $T$ imply
that equality of observations is equivalent to equality of $\xi$ and
$\eta^2$. Factoring $\eta_1^2-\eta_2^2$ proves \eqref{eq:fiber}.
Multiplying the repeated nonzero $z$ by two rotations gives the same
signal precisely when the rotations themselves are equal. The complex
exponential has kernel $2\pi i\mathbb Z$, which proves
\eqref{eq:phase-lock}. At $z=0$ both products vanish.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Two distinct failures of unrestricted recurrence]\label{prop:counterexamples}
The curve $\gamma(\tau)=(\tau,\tau)$ crosses the model fold transversely
at zero, but $(\tau,\tau^2)$ is injective. Even a retraced nonzero
observation need not recur after rotation: at $z=1+i$, phases $0$ and
$\pi$ give different signals.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The derivative of the normal coordinate at zero is one, while equality
of the first output coordinates forces equality of the times.
For the second statement $e^{i\pi}z=-z\ne z$.
\end{proof}
Consequently v4 Theorem 5.3 is replaced, not retroactively certified.
There is no assertion here that an arbitrary nonperiodic trajectory
crossing a fold has a repeated signal, a dominant Fourier frequency,
or a dynamically produced oscillator.
